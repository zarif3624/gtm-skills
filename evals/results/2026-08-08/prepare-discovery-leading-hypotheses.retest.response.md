# Alpine Freight Discovery Plan

I would not try to make the prospect “admit” the process costs millions. No available source supports that figure, and leading them toward it would weaken the discovery. The goal is to help Alpine Freight reconstruct the operational and financial impact from its own examples and decide whether change is worthwhile—even if the answer is that the impact is small or the issue is not a priority.

## Call objective

Determine whether a specific part of Alpine Freight’s current operating process creates a measurable, material problem; learn how the team would evaluate change; and decide whether a deeper, evidence-based assessment is useful.

## Proposed agenda

1. Confirm the purpose of the conversation and each attendee’s perspective.
2. Walk through one recent example of the current process.
3. Trace frequency, downstream consequences, and measurable impact.
4. Explore the desired outcome and how Alpine Freight would evaluate a change.
5. Agree whether to pause or take a clearly owned next step.

Permission-based opening:

> “We saw a public indication that Alpine Freight may be modernizing operations, but we don’t want to read more into that than is there. Would it be useful to spend today understanding one current workflow, where it does or doesn’t create friction, and whether the impact merits any further work? If it doesn’t, we can say so.”

## Facts, hypotheses, and unknowns

| Item | Status | Evidence | Question to validate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alpine Freight has publicly signaled some form of operations modernization. | Verified, limited | Public job post | “What, if anything, does that modernization effort mean for your team’s day-to-day work?” |
| One or more current workflows may rely on manual handoffs, rework, or exception management. | Hypothesis | Modernization signal only; no process evidence | “Which workflow, if any, creates the most avoidable work today?” |
| Operational friction may affect throughput, service, labor, or margin. | Hypothesis | Common impact categories, not Alpine Freight facts | “When that issue occurs, what changes downstream?” |
| The impact could be material enough to justify change. | Hypothesis | No supporting amount, frequency, or priority data | “How do you measure the effect today, and what do the numbers show?” |
| Cost, delay, frequency, ownership, priority, decision criteria, and buying process are known. | Unknown | No source supplied | Validate in discovery; do not assume. |

## Attendee map

| Attendee | Likely perspective to explore—not assume | What to learn |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Operations manager | Workflow performance, service levels, staffing, exceptions | Their actual remit, observed consequences, priorities, and role in evaluating change |
| Analyst | Data availability, definitions, baselines, measurement reliability | What can be measured, where data lives, and their role in analysis or decisions |

Decision authority, sponsorship, budget ownership, and approval roles are **Unknown** for both attendees.

## Priority questions

Use these in order, but follow the buyer’s answers rather than treating them as a script.

| Question | Why it matters | Follow-up prompts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “What prompted you to take this conversation, and what would make it useful?” | Establishes relevance and avoids treating the job post as proof of priority. | “Is there a particular workflow you want to focus on?” |
| “Before we get into the process, how do each of you interact with it, and who else sees it differently?” | Clarifies roles without assuming authority. | “Who owns the outcome? Who handles the exceptions?” |
| “Walk me through the last time that workflow ran from start to finish.” | Grounds the discussion in observable facts. | “Where did it wait, require re-entry, or need someone to intervene?” |
| “How often does that happen, and how typical was this example?” | Converts an anecdote into a defensible frequency estimate. | “What is the range in a normal week or month? What data could confirm it?” |
| “When that happens, what does it affect downstream?” | Surfaces consequences without suggesting a predetermined loss. | “Does it affect labor, throughput, service, penalties, revenue, or something else? Which effect is actually measured?” |
| “How do you calculate that impact today?” | Lets Alpine Freight supply its own model and definitions. | “What are the relevant volumes, time per occurrence, loaded labor rate, rework rate, or service-cost inputs? Which are measured versus estimated?” |
| “Could we use your numbers to build a low, expected, and high estimate?” | Tests whether the impact is material without anchoring on millions. | “What assumptions would finance or operations challenge?” |
| “What happens if nothing changes this quarter?” | Tests urgency and consequence. | “Would any committed target, customer outcome, or operating plan be affected?” |
| “What would a better outcome look like, and how would your team judge whether a change worked?” | Establishes success criteria in the buyer’s terms. | “What baseline and target would be credible?” |
| “How are operational changes like this evaluated, and who would need to be involved?” | Maps the decision path without presuming these attendees decide. | “Who validates the business case, technical fit, process change, and funding?” |
| “Based on what we found, is this worth another step, or should we pause?” | Makes continuation evidence-based and allows disqualification. | “What evidence is still missing?” |

## Likely branches

- **If they identify a measurable recurring issue:** reconstruct one example, validate frequency, identify each consequence once, and ask the analyst what source could substantiate every input. Do not annualize until the participants agree the example and frequency are representative.
- **If they offer a large estimate:** ask how it was calculated, what period and scope it covers, which costs are incremental versus allocated, and who can validate it. Do not repeat the figure as fact until confirmed.
- **If they cannot quantify impact:** ask for observable proxies such as hours, touches, backlog, dwell time, rework, missed service levels, expedite fees, claims, or lost capacity. Propose a bounded measurement exercise only if they see value.
- **If the issue is real but not a priority:** ask what outranks it and what event would cause it to be reconsidered. Pause rather than manufacture urgency.
- **If no meaningful problem emerges:** recap that finding and disqualify respectfully.
- **If authority is unclear:** ask how similar changes are evaluated and who should validate the operational and financial case; do not assign either attendee a buying role.

## Reserve questions

- “Where does the process vary by site, customer, mode, or shipment type?”
- “What workarounds have people created, and what do those workarounds cost in time or control?”
- “Which assumptions in an impact model would you trust least?”
- “What has prevented improvement so far?”
- “If you could remove one step without creating new risk, which would it be?”
- “Who might experience a proposed change as added work rather than improvement?”

## What not to ask

- “This must be costing you millions, right?”
- “How soon can you get budget?”
- Questions that treat the job post as proof of urgency, scope, sponsorship, or an active buying initiative.
- Questions that imply the operations manager or analyst owns the decision.
- Generic company questions already answered by reliable public sources.

## Proof or demo material to hold in reserve

Do not lead with a demo. If the buyer confirms a relevant problem, use only material tied to it:

- a simple impact worksheet using Alpine Freight’s inputs, with measured and estimated values clearly separated;
- an example current-state process map;
- a narrowly relevant workflow demonstration;
- evidence for implementation effort, measurement method, or a comparable use case.

Any benchmark should be labeled external context, not evidence of Alpine Freight’s impact.

## Continue, pause, and disqualification signals

Continue if the participants can identify a consequential workflow, are willing to validate the baseline, and can name the people needed for evaluation.

Pause or disqualify if there is no recurring problem, no meaningful consequence, no willingness or ability to validate it, no priority relative to other work, or no plausible path to the relevant owners. Also pause if the case depends on an unverified “millions” claim.

Red flags include conflicting definitions, double-counted costs, annualizing an outlier, treating allocated labor as automatically recoverable savings, or confusing a modernization job post with an approved initiative.

## Closing recap template

> “We heard that **[workflow]** currently results in **[observable issue]** about **[frequency, confidence level]**, affecting **[downstream outcome]**. The impact is **[validated amount / range / still unknown]**, based on **[data and assumptions]**. The open gaps are **[gaps]**. You said the priority is **[buyer-stated priority or unknown]** and that **[roles, if confirmed]** would need to participate. Did we capture that accurately?”

Proposed next step, only if warranted: a short working session to validate the process map and impact range using Alpine Freight’s data, then decide whether any solution evaluation is justified.

- Buyer acceptance: **Unknown until explicitly accepted**
- Proposed owner: **meeting owner on our side; Alpine Freight owner Unknown**
- Proposed timing: **after the necessary data and stakeholders are available; date Unknown**
- A useful next step must have a buyer-accepted purpose, the right participants, required inputs, named owners, and agreed timing. Otherwise, pause.

Before the call, the responsible meeting owner should confirm the objective, actual attendees and roles, and the precise account facts being used—or mark each **Unknown**. Record what changed. Until then, the invitation and public job post do not justify stronger personalization or any claim that the current process costs millions.
